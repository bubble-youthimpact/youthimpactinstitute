<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Asexuality remains largely invisible in global sexual health policies and research, resulting in inadequate services and social stigma for asexual individuals. This memo proposes targeted steps for WHO to recognize asexuality as a valid orientation, include it in sexual health metrics, and fund research on unique asexual health experiences. Lack of representation in public discourse perpetuates misunderstanding, pathologization, and erasure.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>Public health systems often overlook asexuality, treating it as a deviation rather than an identity.<br>This institutional erasure results in:</p>
<ul><li>Asexual individuals being misdiagnosed with sexual dysfunctions or mental illness.</li><li>Invisibility in surveys, leading to no reliable global prevalence data.</li><li>Little to no training for clinicians on the needs of asexual patients.</li><li>Pathologization of asexuality in mental health frameworks, including confusion with hypoactive sexual desire disorder.</li></ul>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<p>Health systems are organized around assumptions of sexual activity and desire, which marginalizes asexual people:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Diagnostic Overreach:</strong> Clinicians often default to treating asexuality as a problem to be fixed, subjecting individuals to unnecessary interventions.</li><li><strong>Data Absence = Policy Silence:</strong> Without data, asexuality remains excluded from health education, funding allocations, and policy agendas.</li><li><strong>Consent Education Gaps:</strong> Current models don't account for asexual-specific consent frameworks (e.g., “touch aversion,” relational boundaries).</li><li><strong>Intersectional Neglect:</strong> Asexual people who are disabled, non-binary, or neurodivergent face compounded marginalization due to overlapping erasures.</li></ul>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<h3>Recognition and Classification</h3>
<ul><li>Officially recognize asexuality as a valid sexual orientation in all WHO guidelines and documents.</li><li>Differentiate asexuality from clinical disorders related to sexual functioning.</li></ul>
<h3>Inclusion in Data and Research</h3>
<ul><li>Add asexuality-specific questions to global sexual and reproductive health surveys.</li><li>Fund cross-cultural research on health outcomes among asexual populations.</li></ul>
<h3>Medical and Public Education</h3>
<ul><li>Update clinician training materials to include best practices for interacting with asexual patients.</li><li>Launch a global public awareness campaign about asexuality and health rights.</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Without naming asexuality, public health systems perpetuate its erasure. WHO must take leadership to affirm asexual identities, expand inclusive care models, and promote the visibility of underrepresented sexualities. Health equity depends on acknowledging the full spectrum of sexual experiences—including none.</p>
