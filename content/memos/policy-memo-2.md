<p><strong>To</strong>: United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)<br><strong>From</strong>: Sakura Tanaka (Japan), Youth Impact Institute (YII) Policy Research Team<br><strong>Subject</strong>: Preserving Indigenous Languages Through Digital Technology</p>
<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Indigenous languages are vanishing at an alarming rate, with one language disappearing every two weeks. This erosion undermines cultural heritage, biodiversity knowledge, and community identity. This memo proposes a digitally driven approach to language preservation that empowers Indigenous youth and communities. UNESCO can lead a multi-sector strategy combining mobile technology, digital archiving, curriculum development, and intergenerational mentorship to sustain and revitalize Indigenous languages.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>There are over 7,000 languages spoken globally, yet 40% are endangered, with many lacking written forms or formal recognition. Indigenous youth often receive education in dominant languages, leading to language attrition across generations.</p>
<p>For instance:</p>
<ul><li>In the Amazon basin, fewer than 10% of Indigenous children receive instruction in their native language.</li><li>In North America, fewer than 5% of Native youth speak their tribal language fluently.</li><li>UN reports estimate that by 2100, over half of all languages could be extinct without urgent action.</li></ul>
<p>While UNESCO’s “International Decade of Indigenous Languages” has raised awareness, digital tools remain underutilized in revitalization efforts.</p>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<p>Language preservation efforts face key structural barriers:</p>
<ul><li><strong>Lack of digital records</strong>: Many Indigenous languages are oral-only, with little to no written or audiovisual documentation.</li><li><strong>Exclusion from curricula</strong>: National education systems rarely support multilingual or Indigenous instruction.</li><li><strong>Resource bottlenecks</strong>: Language preservation is often confined to underfunded local initiatives.</li><li><strong>Youth disengagement</strong>: Without relevant, tech-enabled language platforms, younger generations lose interest.</li></ul>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<p>UNESCO can launch a global digital preservation campaign via the following:</p>
<h3>Community-Led Digital Archiving</h3>
<ul><li>Partner with tribal councils and linguists to record and digitize stories, songs, and dictionaries.</li><li>Fund open-access language platforms co-managed by communities.</li></ul>
<h3>EdTech for Multilingual Learning</h3>
<ul><li>Support the development of language learning apps, subtitles, and digital games in endangered languages.</li><li>Facilitate integration of Indigenous languages in school e-curricula.</li></ul>
<h3>Youth Training and Mentorship</h3>
<ul><li>Create youth-led language clubs with elder mentorship and digital storytelling projects.</li><li>Fund fellowships for young Indigenous technologists to design culturally relevant tools.</li></ul>
<h3>Global Awareness Campaigns</h3>
<ul><li>Mobilize UNESCO’s media channels to celebrate Indigenous language champions and creators.</li><li>Include Indigenous language recognition in UNESCO World Heritage programs.</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Indigenous language survival is a matter of cultural justice and identity preservation. Through digital innovation and respectful collaboration, UNESCO can empower Indigenous communities and youth to reclaim and revitalize their linguistic heritage in a rapidly changing world.</p>
