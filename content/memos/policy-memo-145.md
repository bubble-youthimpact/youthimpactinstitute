<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Young workers, especially in remote and digital industries, are increasingly subject to round-the-clock work expectations that blur the line between labor and leisure. A National Right-to-Disconnect law would protect employees from after-hours digital communication, supporting mental health and work-life balance. This memo outlines a federal labor standard prohibiting employer retaliation when workers decline to engage in off-the-clock communication. Protecting the right to disconnect is critical for a sustainable, fair, and youth-friendly economy.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>Digital technologies have created “always-on” work cultures, particularly for youth in hybrid or creative industries. Without clear protections, young employees feel pressure to remain responsive late into the night. European democracies have passed right-to-disconnect legislation to address digital burnout, but the U.S. lacks federal standards.</p>
<ul><li>68% of Gen Z workers report experiencing burnout, often due to unclear work boundaries.</li><li>Studies show overwork correlates with anxiety, sleep deprivation, and reduced productivity.</li><li>Existing labor laws do not address digital communication norms.</li><li>France, Italy, and the Philippines have implemented national right-to-disconnect frameworks.</li><li>Gig and freelance youth are especially vulnerable to retaliation or loss of future contracts.</li></ul>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<ul><li>Legal boundaries protect mental health and reduce turnover.</li><li>Disconnected time is essential for creativity, learning, and family responsibilities.</li><li>Small businesses may need flexibility, requiring exemptions or phase-in options.</li><li>Employer pushback can be mitigated with education and productivity studies.</li><li>Remote and creative economies need new protections tailored to digital realities.</li></ul>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<h3>Amend the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)</h3>
<ul><li>Add protections against retaliation for workers who do not engage in off-the-clock digital communication.</li><li>Define employer obligations for establishing “disconnect hours” in employment contracts.</li></ul>
<h3>Create a Federal Disconnect Advisory</h3>
<ul><li>Require the Department of Labor to issue guidance on best practices by sector (e.g., media, tech, education).</li><li>Encourage employers to post “digital disengagement” policies publicly.</li></ul>
<h3>Launch a Youth Work-Life Balance Pilot</h3>
<ul><li>Fund pilot programs in youth-dominated sectors (e.g., creator economy, non-profits) to model best practices.</li><li>Measure impacts on retention, wellbeing, and productivity.</li></ul>
<h3>Support State Innovation</h3>
<ul><li>Provide grants to states that develop creative implementation strategies (e.g., app notifications blackout zones, auto-responder protocols).</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>The future of work must include the freedom to disconnect. As youth face record mental health challenges and work-life imbalances, Congress must recognize that being “reachable” is not the same as being available. A National Right-to-Disconnect law would help redefine labor for the digital age—supporting health, equity, and sustainable productivity.</p>
