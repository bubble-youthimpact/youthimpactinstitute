<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Access to quality education is a basic human right, yet incarcerated youth are routinely denied meaningful educational opportunities. This memo urges UNESCO to spearhead an international initiative that guarantees consistent, accredited, and rehabilitative education for youth in detention, including digital access, teacher training, and reentry support.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>Globally, hundreds of thousands of minors are held in juvenile detention centers, many without access to formal schooling. Educational neglect within these institutions worsens long-term outcomes, entrenching cycles of poverty and recidivism.<br>Key trends include:</p>
<ul><li>In low-income countries, over 60% of incarcerated youth report no access to secondary education.</li><li>Teachers in youth facilities often lack training in trauma-informed or special education pedagogy.</li><li>Reentry into mainstream education is rarely supported, resulting in high dropout rates post-release.</li></ul>
<p>The absence of education during incarceration violates international human rights norms and undermines rehabilitation efforts.</p>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<p>Challenges include:</p>
<ul><li>Infrastructural Deficits: Many facilities lack classrooms, learning materials, or internet access.</li><li><strong>Untrained Educators:</strong> Teachers in detention centers often lack certification or experience.</li><li><strong>Disconnected Curricula:</strong> Programs are not aligned with national education systems, limiting reintegration.</li><li><strong>Stigma and Exclusion:</strong> Post-release students face discrimination from schools and employers.</li></ul>
<p>Without intervention, incarcerated youth are pushed further from education rather than reconnected to it.</p>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<p>UNESCO should:</p>
<h3>Accredited Curriculum Access</h3>
<ul><li>Develop a flexible, modular educational curriculum tailored to youth detention contexts.</li><li>Provide low-cost digital learning tools and materials for under-resourced facilities.</li></ul>
<h3>Teacher Training</h3>
<ul><li>Offer trauma-informed and rights-based training for educators in detention centers.</li><li>Establish global educator networks to share resources and support.</li></ul>
<h3>Reintegration Support</h3>
<ul><li>Promote programs that bridge the transition from detention to formal education.</li><li>Fund scholarships and mentorship for formerly incarcerated youth.</li></ul>
<h3>Data and Accountability</h3>
<ul><li>Track and publish data on educational access in detention across member states.</li><li>Set international minimum standards for youth prison education systems.</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Education in youth detention must be rehabilitative, not punitive. By coordinating global efforts and enforcing accountability, UNESCO can restore educational rights to the most vulnerable and create pathways to dignity and opportunity.</p>
