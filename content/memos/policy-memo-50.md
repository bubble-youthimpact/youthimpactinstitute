<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Youth leadership spaces—UN youth forums, educational exchange programs, policymaking fellowships—often exclude disabled youth through inaccessible applications, tokenistic engagement, and implicit bias. UNESCO must reform its youth initiatives to become truly inclusive and representative of the diversity of youth experiences.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>From UNESCO youth summits to Model UNs and policy hackathons, youth leadership is increasingly celebrated—but structurally ableist. Applications often assume verbal, written, or travel ability. Events rarely offer interpreters, captioning, or sensory accommodations. These barriers prevent youth with disabilities from shaping the very policies that affect them.</p>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<ul><li><strong>Structural Exclusion:</strong> Leadership pipelines assume able-bodied norms of productivity, visibility, and speed.</li><li><strong>Representation Gap:</strong> Youth policy spaces often feature one token disabled delegate rather than systemic inclusion.</li><li><strong>Funding Inaccessibility:</strong> Travel costs, personal care needs, and assistance are rarely covered.</li><li><strong>Cultural Erasure:</strong> Disabled leadership styles and communication forms are undervalued.</li></ul>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<h3>Universal Accessibility in Youth Programs</h3>
<ul><li>Make accessibility a prerequisite for all UNESCO-affiliated youth programs.</li><li>Offer application flexibility (video, alternative formats, extended deadlines).</li></ul>
<h3>Representative Leadership Models</h3>
<ul><li>Require diverse disability representation in youth advisory councils.</li><li>Fund peer mentorship networks among disabled youth leaders.</li></ul>
<h3>Access Funding and Accommodations</h3>
<ul><li>Create travel and assistance scholarships for youth with disabilities.</li><li>Require hosting organizations to provide full access accommodations.</li></ul>
<h3>Cultural Change and Capacity Building</h3>
<ul><li>Train UNESCO staff and youth partners in disability justice principles.</li><li>Promote disabled youth leadership not as an exception, but as a norm.</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Disability inclusion is not about adding one seat at the table—it’s about redesigning the table. UNESCO has a responsibility to ensure youth leadership reflects the full spectrum of human experience and ability.</p>
