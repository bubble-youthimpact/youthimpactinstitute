<h3>Executive Summary</h3>
<p>Comprehensive Sexuality Education (CSE) remains politically contested, underfunded, and inconsistently implemented across the Global South. Yet, it is vital for protecting youth from misinformation, coercion, and discrimination. This memo calls on UNFPA to lead a rights-based expansion of CSE by investing in culturally relevant curricula, local educator training, and mechanisms to safeguard inclusive content from political backlash.</p>
<h3>Background</h3>
<p>CSE equips young people with knowledge and skills around consent, identity, reproduction, and healthy relationships. Despite global recognition in frameworks, such as the ICPD Programme of Action, over half of the world's youth live in countries where sexuality education is abstinence-only or nonexistent. Marginalized youth—including LGBTQ+, disabled, and Indigenous adolescents—face the greatest information gaps. The COVID-19 pandemic further disrupted delivery, compounding vulnerability to gender-based violence and unintended pregnancy.</p>
<h3>Analysis</h3>
<p>The global push for CSE faces significant resistance rooted in moral panic, cultural taboos, and political opportunism.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Cultural Weaponization:</strong> Political leaders often frame CSE as foreign imposition, stirring nationalism and anti-LGBTQ+ sentiment.</li><li><strong>Legal Ambiguity:</strong> In many countries, no legal framework protects students’ right to sexual health information.</li><li><strong>Digital Displacement:</strong> Online CSE has surged, but without regulation, it risks reproducing biases and lacks reach among low-income youth.</li><li><strong>Educator Disempowerment:</strong> Teachers report fear of job loss or community backlash for discussing identity, menstruation, or contraception.</li><li><strong>Curricular Blind Spots:</strong> Existing programs often omit queer relationships, intersex bodies, and trauma-informed approaches.</li></ul>
<p>Unless sexuality education is reframed not as moral risk but as structural empowerment, CSE will remain vulnerable to rollback.</p>
<h3>Recommendations</h3>
<h3>Rights-Based Curricula</h3>
<ul><li>Promote youth co-designed, trauma-informed, and queer-inclusive CSE frameworks.</li><li>Integrate content on pleasure, consent, and diverse identities.</li></ul>
<h3>Educator Protection</h3>
<ul><li>Fund legal support and professional networks for teachers delivering inclusive CSE.</li><li>Partner with unions to advocate for safe working conditions.</li></ul>
<h3>Policy Safeguards</h3>
<ul><li>Push national ministries to codify CSE as a youth right under national education laws.</li><li>Offer incentives for governments to pilot regional CSE hubs.</li></ul>
<h3>Localized Innovation</h3>
<ul><li>Support NGOs creating culturally adapted media and storytelling approaches to CSE.</li><li>Fund mobile-friendly CSE platforms for offline youth communities.</li></ul>
<h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Sexuality education is not just about bodies—it’s about dignity, freedom, and structural empowerment. By supporting youth-driven, politically resilient CSE strategies, UNFPA can help shift the global conversation from protectionism to rights realization.</p>
