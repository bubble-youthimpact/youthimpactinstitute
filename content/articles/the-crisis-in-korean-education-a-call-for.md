<p>In the realm of education, contemporary South Korean society finds itself in a state of profound crisis. While Korean students are renowned for their diligence and academic prowess, a closer examination reveals a deeply troubling paradox. Despite their arduous efforts, these students rank dismally low in measures of happiness, job satisfaction, and overall life contentment when compared to their international counterparts. OECD shows that the level of that is almost the level of OECD's lowest countries. However, even these visible metrics merely scratch the surface of a much deeper, systemic issue.</p>
<p>The current state of Korean education can be aptly described as deeply pathological, steering students' life trajectories in fundamentally misguided directions. The structure of higher education admission in Korea is primarily bifurcated into two paths: regular admission (CSAT) and early admission (school exam based). Both methods are heavily reliant on standardized testing, with regular admission being entirely test-based and early admission incorporating school performance and teacher recommendations, albeit still test scores in school taking the greatest significance. This system is designed to sort and distribute students among universities based on their test performance.</p>
<p>However, this structure, when combined with an excessive and obsessive zeal for academic achievement and a societally entrenched obsession for university prestige, has transmuted the educational process into one that prioritizes test preparation above all else. The curriculum has become unnecessarily challenging, focused on producing high test scores rather than fostering genuine learning and personal growth. There is a big difference between studying for tests and studying for scholastic achievement and learning. While the scholastic study is characterized by questioning and exploring, study for tests is absolute. Studying for better test scores almost resembles an AI machine learning process, where student injects content for study.</p>
<p>To truly understand the gravity of the situation, it is important to examine the underlying pervasive societal ideology that forms this educational paradigm. When high school students, who study until 10 PM daily, are asked about their motivations, the majority respond with a utilitarian calculus: studying is necessary for university admission, which in turn is crucial for securing employment and financial stability. As simply studying hard to get a better score on tests directly relates to better college admission results, the majority are just interested in getting high scores to go to a better college to get a better job. This whole system of education makes the student think that their life will be better once they got in to a better college, and sets their whole goal of life as a student to a better college and, specifically, a better test result. The majority believe that a university degree is the primary, if not the only, pathway to societal success and contribution. Thus, without examining the true value of education, they seek mostly to get a better score.</p>
<p>The materialistic ideology of modern Korean society dramatically worsens this effect, as the situation becomes even more alarming when considering high-achieving students. A significant portion of top-performing students aspire to enter medical school, motivated primarily by the promise of a stable and lucrative career. This trend is so pronounced that even students in specialized science high schools, who receive substantial government funding, were willing to forfeit their scholarships to pursue medical studies until legal regulations were put in place to prevent this. Even within the medical field itself, some specialties perceived as less financially rewarding struggle to attract candidates, leading to workforce shortages.</p>
<p>This fixation on material success can be seen even at an alarmingly young age when some parents make their elementary kids, who are too young even to know themselves, attend private "pre-med" classes that aim to study for admission to medical school. Moreover, the popularity of various fields of study in College is overwhelmingly determined by their potential for financial gain. This widespread materialism is starkly illustrated by an OECD survey, in which South Korea was the only country to rank money as the most valuable aspect of life. In fact, this materialistic obsession exists throughout society beyond education. Comparison between people based on wealth and excessive emphasis on monetary conditions in the marriage market, and so on. The educational phenomenon is just a partial reflection of this pervasive materialism in society. A society centered around such materialistic ideals is inherently incapable of achieving true happiness. Material wealth is finite and can only be abundantly possessed by a minority. A society that places material acquisition as its primary goal is destined to grapple with the tragedy of material insufficiency, never satisfying everyone eager to possess wealth. However, this raises a question: Is it the materialistic ideology that shapes the education system, or does the flawed educational structure engender a materialistic worldview by offering limited life paths? While the exact causal relationship may be difficult to determine, it is evident that these factors are mutually reinforcing, with the compulsory education system playing a significant role in exacerbating this materialistic ideology.</p>
<p>The current Korean educational system offers only a narrow range of post-secondary pathways, primarily centered around standardized testing. This myopic structure is fundamentally problematic. The notion of evaluating millions of unique individuals through a single, uniform method and determining their futures based on these results is deeply flawed and fails to recognize the diversity of human potential and aptitude. This unidirectional system forces students who are not naturally inclined towards academic pursuits to spend years struggling with subjects that may have little relevance to their true potential, enduring meaningless suffering in the process. It's crucial to redefine the purpose of education, particularly compulsory education from 1-12 grade. Its aim should not be to categorize and rank individuals for university admission, but to help each person discover and maximize their unique potential.</p>
<p>To address these issues, a comprehensive reform of the education system is necessary. One potential model to consider is the extracurricular activity system prevalent in American college admissions. This system allows students to demonstrate their interests and passions through various activities outside of academic coursework. Implementing a similar system in Korea, with schools offering structured extracurricular programs, could provide students with opportunities to explore diverse fields and discover their true interests.</p>
<p>Additionally, expanding the range of elective subjects offered in schools could allow students to delve deeper into areas that align with their potential and interests. This approach would shift the focus from test preparation to personal development and skill acquisition. It's important to note that systemic changes alone may not be sufficient without a corresponding shift in societal values. If the majority of students continue to pursue careers solely based on financial considerations, even an ideally restructured education system may struggle to achieve its intended outcomes.</p>
<p>Nevertheless, such reforms would likely benefit the majority of students, particularly those in the middle academic tier who currently study without clear purpose or direction. Furthermore, even for those who initially prioritize material success, a more diverse and personalized educational experience may eventually lead to a reevaluation of their values and goals.</p>
<p>In conclusion, while the task of reforming the Korean education system and shifting societal values is undoubtedly challenging, it is not insurmountable. Throughout history, human ideologies have evolved, with truth consistently prevailing over falsehood. By gradually reorienting the prevalent pathological ideologies in society, we can aspire to create an educational system that truly serves the diverse needs and potentials of all students, ultimately leading to a more fulfilled and balanced society.</p>
