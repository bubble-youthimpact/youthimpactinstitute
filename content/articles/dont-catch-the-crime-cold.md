<p>Recall your childhood memory. One kid coughs a few times during math class, and - before you know it - your friends get infected one by one as if it tipped off a sort of domino chain.</p>
<p>Like most of you would know, this is typically how a contagious disease spreads - but here is the scary part: this dynamic has parallels in crimes.</p>
<p>The mass shooting contagion theory, for example, states that the media coverage of mass shootings increases the likelihood of related crimes. This theory, coined by the sociologist David Philips, is related to the behavior contagion, which contends that a certain behavior can gradually permeate a large group. This phenomenon resembles the spread of a disease like a cold, but its consequences seem much darker and bleaker than a few coughs.</p>
<p>Korea recently had multiple cases of stabbing incidents, which brought intense terror into the country and the minds of its citizens. On 21 July, perhaps the spark that started it all, a 30-year-old man attacked 4 men with a knife in Sillim Station, leaving 3 of them to be severely injured and one of them to be dead. A string of related crimes then followed such as the Seohyeon Station incident, which resulted in the tragic death of 2 men.</p>
<p>“Korea is so scary these days,” cried many citizens online. Fear filled the streets of Korea, and people - when outside - had to bear in mind the terrifying possibility that the people around them might be hiding a knife behind their back.</p>
<p>The distress might have been amplified by the foreignness of such brutal terrorist acts, for most Koreans are accustomed to the safe atmosphere of their community. The country has one of the highest safety indexes in the world, and the citizens are purportedly all so bound by a tacit chain of trust that one can even boldly venture to leave one’s phone in a public area without having to worry about it getting stolen.</p>
<p>Amid the horror, the Korean authorities have obviously provided strict measures to mitigate the frightening atmosphere of the country.</p>
<p>Yoon Suk Yeol, the president of South Korea, ordered for a strict course of actions by commenting, “As threatening texts were also posted on social media, the government should respond by deploying security personnel for prevention and carrying effective and powerful suppression equipment.”</p>
<p>Seung Jae Hyun, a senior research fellow at the Korean Institute of Criminology and Justice, pointed out the role of the media in fueling the terrorist acts by noting, “It is very dangerous to report too provocatively or to report without filtering out the story of the suspect...This can encourage copycat crimes.”</p>
<p>Indeed, the media seems to play a significant part in prompting a series of seemingly similar crimes. Considering the aforementioned theory, the coverage of terrorist acts can spark the fire that might be lying dormant inside a potential criminal, which is certainly not desirable. After all, we don’t want the “crime flu” to be plaguing the streets of South Korea.</p>
<p>Although the previous flare of the crimes has now relatively diminished as of October, there are still a few horrifying episodes of stabbing crimes around the country. The government and the media would need to work together to provide appropriate measures in order to create a safe environment for the Korean citizens.</p>
