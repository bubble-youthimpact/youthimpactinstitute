<p>In 2015, 21 young Americans sued the United States for continuously permitting, authorizing, and subsidizing fossil fuel extraction and consumption, despite these activities’ contributions to global warming (Harvard Law Review, 2021). The coalition argued that the state’s failure to meaningfully combat climate change compromised the constitutional guarantee of “life, liberty, and property” in the present—and, more importantly, for future generations (Harvard Law Review, 2021).</p>
<p>If we evaluate their case temporally, which was later thrown out by the Supreme Court, we see that the youth coalition was rallying for the speculative constitutional violation of their descendants’ right to a “stable climate system” in the present. In response, lawyers for the United States argued that the plaintiffs “had not stated a claim for which relief could be granted” (Harvard Law Review, 2021).</p>
<p>There are two key takeaways from the aforementioned Juliana v. United States case: increasingly, legislative and policymaking paradigms are accounting for a future that appears distressingly uncertain, and in turn, these paradigms are driving major shifts in how we define moral and urgent needs in the present—often framed as “short-termism.” In climate mitigation discourse, “short-termism,” or our fixation on the present, has taken on a negative connotation, given the reckless, almost callous exploitation of resources that threatens the planet’s near-term habitability. It is often argued that short-term gains, such as fossil-fuel-driven economic growth, must be sacrificed for the well-being of future generations.</p>
<p>A crusade against short-termism carries a sense of altruism that departs from conventional moral temporality. Philosophers like Hilary Greaves argue that we should remain agnostic to temporal distance when evaluating our responsibilities to future lives (Samuel, 2022). This raises a fundamental philosophical question: given our distance from future generations, is it morally justified to prioritize their speculative needs over our own?</p>
<p>Importantly, this question is posed within the current global context—one marked by systemic inequality in resource allocation, uneven climate vulnerability, neo-colonial exploitation of the “periphery,” and weakening welfare systems. Considering the severe consequences of these structures on people today and the freedoms they restrict, it can be argued that prioritizing the present generation is morally sufficient (Samuel, 2021). Yet, discussions often fixate on the moral failings of short-termism without fully addressing its urgency.</p>
<p>The central thesis here is that we owe the present generation a short-term policy focus, one that is not subordinated to wealth-oriented longtermist frameworks. The fundamental divide between short-termism and longtermism lies in the emergence of “disposable lives”—those deemed sacrificial for the sake of a distant future.</p>
<h2>The Moral Urgency of Short-Termism</h2>
<p>The moral urgency behind securing freedoms in the short term does not stem from a disregard for the future. Rather, it arises from the moral shortcomings of longtermism, particularly its implicit acceptance of present-day disposability. Supported by influential figures like Elon Musk (Torres, 2024b), longtermism has emerged as a growing intellectual movement, notably from Oxford University, with key contributions from William MacAskill, author of What We Owe to the Future (Samuel, 2022).</p>
<p>At its core, longtermism posits that our highest ethical obligation today is to increase—even marginally—the probability of humanity’s long-term survival and expansion across the cosmos (Zaitchik, 2022). From a utilitarian perspective, this implies that finite resources must be allocated toward maximizing long-term outcomes, often at the cost of present-day needs (Zaitchik, 2022). These trade-offs are justified on the grounds that future generations could produce vastly greater value, both in number and capability, rendering current suffering comparatively insignificant.</p>
<p>MacAskill frames this as overcoming “the tyranny of the present over the future.” If taken seriously, longtermism would require policymaking and resource allocation to prioritize outcomes billions of years ahead. While this may initially seem abstract or counterintuitive, its growing influence in contemporary discourse suggests otherwise.</p>
<p>Taken to its logical conclusion, longtermism could deprioritize causes that do not pose existential threats to humanity. Climate change, for example, might be seen as less urgent if it does not eliminate humanity entirely—since some future population would likely survive (Torres, 2024b). Under this reasoning, it could be considered morally acceptable to divert funding from clean energy initiatives in developing regions toward speculative scientific research, such as the search for exoplanets.</p>
<p>However, these hypothetical scenarios are not neutral in their implications. The burdens of longtermist trade-offs are not distributed equally. Developed economies—having already benefited from extensive resource exploitation—are better positioned to advocate for long-term priorities. Meanwhile, developing regions face immediate survival challenges.</p>
<p>The Global North, in particular, has the privilege of entertaining longtermist frameworks without confronting existential trade-offs between basic needs and speculative futures. Resource inequality not only shapes climate vulnerability but also determines which lives are treated as expendable. Nick Beckstead, a key figure in longtermist thought, has argued that prioritizing lives in wealthier countries may be justified because they are more likely to influence the long-term future (Eevee &amp; Ruthgrace, 2022).</p>
<p>Taken seriously, this perspective risks legitimizing policies that disproportionately undervalue lives in poorer regions, reinforcing existing global inequalities.</p>
<h2>Policymaking Under Longtermism</h2>
<p>In Precarious Life, Judith Butler asks: “Who counts as human? Whose lives count as lives?” While originally framed in the context of post-9/11 violence, this question extends to broader considerations of whose lives are protected and whose are deemed expendable.</p>
<p>Longtermism risks deepening these disparities by creating conditions in which already vulnerable populations bear the cost of speculative future gains. Poorer countries, which are disproportionately affected by climate change, may be asked to divert scarce resources toward uncertain long-term objectives, exacerbating present-day suffering.</p>
<p>Moral and political institutions rely on the principle that all lives hold equal value. Longtermism challenges this principle by assigning differential value based on factors such as geography, economic status, and perceived future impact. In doing so, it undermines foundational commitments to justice and equality.</p>
<p>Beyond its ethical implications, longtermism also shapes policymaking in ways that may produce tangible harm. For instance, some longtermist perspectives oppose regulation of emerging technologies like AI, prioritizing hypothetical future risks over immediate societal impacts. Effective policymaking, however, requires evaluating policies against realistic counterfactuals and balancing present costs with future benefits.</p>
<p>As Gürtler (2024) notes, policymakers routinely “discount” future outcomes to account for uncertainty, inflation, and opportunity costs. This reflects a practical recognition that not all future scenarios carry equal weight. Prioritizing short-term policies, therefore, is not short-sighted—it is necessary for preventing structural collapse, particularly for vulnerable populations.</p>
<p>A clear example is the unregulated use of freshwater by AI data centers for cooling, especially in the United States (Nicoletti et al., 2025). As demand for generative AI grows, companies are expanding infrastructure into regions already experiencing water scarcity. Yet regulatory frameworks remain underdeveloped.</p>
<p>From a longtermist perspective, such concerns may be secondary to the potential of AI to solve future global challenges. However, this assumes technological success that is far from guaranteed. As David Thorstad (2023) argues, even if an action is more likely to produce positive outcomes in the distant future, the cumulative uncertainty over time renders such benefits negligible.</p>
<p>Given this uncertainty, prioritizing speculative long-term gains over immediate needs becomes difficult to justify. In contrast, short-term policy interventions—such as regulating resource use—offer tangible and measurable benefits. This approach extends to other critical domains, including education and healthcare.</p>
<p>Ultimately, focusing on the present does not preclude concern for the future. Rather, it ensures that efforts to secure long-term sustainability do not come at the expense of those already most at risk. If only a fraction of humanity survives future crises, as some longtermist projections suggest, then the question remains: at what moral cost is that survival achieved?</p>
