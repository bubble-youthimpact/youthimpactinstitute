Is a Recession Coming in 2025?

Minseo Kim·April 11, 2025·3 min read

The U.S. economy is entering a period of heightened uncertainty, with increasing signs pointing toward a potential recession. Key economic indicators, alongside rising geopolitical tensions and aggressive trade measures, are sparking concerns among economists, investors, and policymakers alike. The recent escalation in tariff policies and their ripple effects across global markets have added new pressure to an already fragile economic outlook.

Tariff Policies Stir Economic Concerns

The U.S. government, under President Donald Trump, has imposed substantial tariffs on a wide range of imported goods, including a sharp increase in duties on Chinese products—some rising as high as 125%. These policies aim to protect domestic industries and encourage U.S.-based manufacturing. However, they have also triggered a series of retaliatory actions from China and other global trading partners.

The back-and-forth tariffs have disrupted established supply chains and contributed to price increases on everyday consumer goods. Economists warn that prolonged trade disputes could lead to reduced business investment, higher costs for manufacturers, and slower economic growth. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce has also voiced concern that these policies, while well-intentioned, may ultimately harm more sectors than they help.

Diverging Views on Recession Probability

The probability of a U.S. recession in the coming months remains a subject of debate, though many financial institutions are adjusting their forecasts upward.

Goldman Sachs recently raised its estimate of a 2025 recession to 45%, pointing to the dampening effect of trade restrictions on both consumer confidence and corporate planning.

J.P. Morgan places the risk at 60%, highlighting “extreme U.S. policies” that they believe could tip the balance from resilience to contraction.

Deutsche Bank, in its latest investor survey, reports that nearly 50% of respondents believe a recession is likely within the next 12 months, reflecting rising uncertainty and caution in financial markets.

These diverging forecasts underscore the unpredictable nature of the current economic environment.

Labor Market Sends Mixed Signals

Despite signs of stress elsewhere in the economy, the U.S. labor market remains relatively robust—at least on the surface. In March, 228,000 jobs were added across various sectors, including healthcare, hospitality, and construction. However, the national unemployment rate inched up to 4.2%, compared to 4.1% the previous month, suggesting some softening in hiring momentum.

Additionally, weekly jobless claims have seen a slight uptick, hinting at potential early warning signs. If job growth slows significantly in the coming months, consumer spending—a major driver of the U.S. economy—could begin to decline.

Investor Sentiment and Market Performance

The financial markets have reacted cautiously to the ongoing uncertainty. While not yet in panic mode, investor sentiment has weakened. On Thursday, the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) dipped by 0.0436% to $524.58, while the Nasdaq-100 ETF (QQQ) fell 0.0423% to $446.18. Analysts note that concerns over how tariffs might affect corporate earnings are beginning to weigh on valuations.

Many institutional investors are adjusting their portfolios, moving toward safer assets like Treasury bonds and gold. Meanwhile, volatility indices have risen modestly, signaling growing nervousness among traders.

Outlook and Considerations

While some economists emphasize that the U.S. economy still shows signs of strength—such as solid consumer spending and resilient job creation—the combination of aggressive trade strategies, inflationary pressures, and slowing global demand is fueling recession fears.

The Federal Reserve has hinted at possible interest rate adjustments if conditions worsen, though its ability to soften a trade-driven downturn may be limited. Policymakers now face the challenge of balancing inflation control with growth preservation.

Going forward, close monitoring of consumer confidence, corporate earnings reports, and global trade developments will be essential to understanding where the economy is headed.
