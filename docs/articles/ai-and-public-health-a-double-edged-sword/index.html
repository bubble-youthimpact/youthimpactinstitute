AI and Public Health: A Double-Edged Sword for Global Wellbeing

Junyoung Baek·October 01, 2025·6 min read

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into healthcare systems promises unprecedented advances in how populations access, receive, and benefit from public health interventions. Sophisticated machine learning models are now diagnosing conditions with superhuman accuracy, predicting outbreaks sooner, and streamlining treatments in ways unimaginable just years ago. The AI healthcare market, valued at $27 billion in 2024, is projected to reach $675 billion by 2034, reflecting explosive growth that mirrors the technology's transformative potential. Yet as AI technologies become more central, these powerful tools also expose deep vulnerabilities and ethical dilemmas—UnitedHealth care’s use of the nH Predict AI algorithm has led to a significant rise in coverage denials for patients needing post-acute care, often overriding physician recommendations and leaving many individuals without access to necessary medical services. The same systems driving medical breakthroughs are also projected to cause 1,300 premature deaths annually in the U.S. by 2030 through air pollution from data centers, imposing nearly $20 billion in health costs. As society stands at a crossroads between breakthrough potential and real-world risks, the essential challenge is ensuring that AI improves public health for everyone, rather than creating new harms or deepening inequities. Therefore, artificial intelligence brings important benefits such as improved disease detection, personalized treatment, and expanded healthcare access, but also presents challenges including cybersecurity risks, algorithmic bias, loss of human oversight, and unequal access; balancing these benefits and drawbacks is crucial for creating a resilient and equitable public health system.

Artificial intelligence is transforming public health by sharpening disease detection, accelerating drug development, and extending care to underserved communities, creating tangible pathways to better outcomes at scale. In diagnostics, models such as a Harvard linked CHIEF system report about 94% accuracy across multiple cancers, signaling earlier and more precise identification than traditional workflows and enabling faster, targeted treatment decisions that improve prognosis. Cardiovascular applications similarly leverage machine learning analysis of ECGs (electrocardiogram) and longitudinal health data to flag risk before symptoms, achieving high classification accuracy and facilitating preventive interventions that reduce morbidity and mortality. In therapeutics, pipeline analyses indicate AI discovered drugs post markedly higher Phase I success (roughly 80–90%) compared with historical rates for conventionally discovered compounds (about 40–65%), implying a step change in research and development productivity as well as the potential to shorten time to effective treatments for patients. Health system deployments round out the promise: AI enabled remote monitoring programs have substantially lowered readmissions—Mayo Clinic’s implementation of AI aftercare system reports around a 40% reduction—while improving continuity of care for patients far from specialty centers, illustrating how intelligent tools can expand access and quality simultaneously.

Artificial intelligence in public health brings with it significant challenges and risks, many of which have the potential to deepen global health disparities rather than resolve them. One of the most visible dangers is bias in AI systems: algorithms are often trained on data gathered predominantly from Western populations, meaning that diagnostic models or treatment recommendations may perform poorly—or even dangerously—when applied in Africa, Asia, or among other underrepresented groups. Skin disease detection models, for example, have shown up to 40% drops in accuracy when tested on darker skin tones, directly impacting patient care in non-Western regions. These problems are not limited to race or geography; gender gaps persist too, with male-centric data sets causing tools to miss symptoms typical in female patients. Beyond bias, cost and inequality remain major barriers: wealthy nations and hospitals enjoy the benefits of frequent AI system updates, advanced hardware, and continuous staff training, while low-resource settings struggle to keep pace and risk falling further behind. The reliance on vast amounts of personal health data for AI tools also raises privacy concerns—with more sensitive information in digital form, the threat of misuse, cyberattack, or patient re-identification is ever-present. And finally, there’s the risk of over-reliance: while algorithms can sift data far faster than humans, depending too much on technology may erode essential human judgment, empathy, and nuance in patient care, especially during complex or emotionally charged cases.

These challenges fuel urgent ethical and equity dilemmas that must be addressed as AI becomes ever more central to public health decision-making. Fundamental questions about data ownership linger: who controls the massive repositories of health information fed into AI patients, hospitals, private tech companies, or governments and how can that control be exercised responsibly and transparently? Without clear regulation, there's a risk that commercially driven systems prioritize profit over fairness, or that marginalized groups lose agency in the use of data extracted from their communities. Ensuring that AI benefits all people not only those in wealthier countries or well-funded clinics demands intentional efforts toward inclusivity at every stage, from data collection and algorithm development to deployment and monitoring. Transparency, accountability, and clear international regulation are vital; right now, few standards exist for how medical AI systems are audited or corrected when errors and bias are discovered. This makes robust public scrutiny, ethical review boards, and global collaboration essential for preventing the creation of a two-tiered health system that privileges those with more resources at the expense of the disadvantaged. Only by confronting these equity challenges and investing in open, fair, and supervised use of AI can public health systems harness technology’s benefits without amplifying its harms.

Realizing AI's potential while mitigating its dangers requires coordinated action from governments, international organizations, and healthcare stakeholders to establish robust governance frameworks that prioritize equity and human oversight. The World Health Organization has already released comprehensive guidance calling for governments to invest in public infrastructure, including computing power and diverse datasets accessible to developers who commit to ethical principles, while mandating rigorous post-deployment auditing and impact assessments to ensure AI systems perform equitably across different demographic groups. Crucially, inclusive datasets that represent diverse populations must become the foundation of AI development current initiatives like the STANDING Together program emphasize that healthcare AI should incorporate comprehensive data collection across age, gender, ethnicity, disability, income, and geographic factors to prevent the perpetuation of existing health disparities. Equally important is maintaining AI as a support tool rather than a replacement for healthcare professionals; the American College of Physicians explicitly recommends that AI should augment, not supplant, physician decision-making, with clinicians retaining ultimate accountability for all patient care decisions. This human-centered approach ensures that while algorithms can process vast amounts of data and identify patterns, the essential elements of empathy, ethical reasoning, and complex clinical judgment that define quality healthcare remain firmly in human hands.

Artificial intelligence in public health is indeed a double-edged sword, capable of revolutionizing global health outcomes while simultaneously threatening to deepen the very inequities it promises to solve. The evidence is clear: AI can dramatically improve diagnostic accuracy, accelerate life-saving drug development, and extend quality care to previously underserved populations, yet these same systems can perpetuate bias, compromise privacy, and create new vulnerabilities that put entire health networks at risk. The challenge facing our generation is not whether AI will reshape public health, that transformation is already underway but rather how we will guide this powerful technology to serve humanity's best interests. If deployed with careful attention to equity, transparency, and human oversight, AI has the potential to create a more just and effective global health system that benefits all populations regardless of geography or economic status. However, if we allow commercial interests, technological determinism, or regulatory neglect to drive AI adoption without adequate safeguards, we risk creating a two-tiered system that amplifies existing disparities and undermines the foundational principles of public health. The choice before us is clear: we can harness AI's remarkable capabilities to build a healthier, more equitable world, or we can allow its unchecked deployment to fragment healthcare along digital divides. The challenge for our generation is not whether AI will shape public health, but how we will shape AI for the health of all.
